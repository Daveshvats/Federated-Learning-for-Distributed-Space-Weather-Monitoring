Table~\ref{tab:main} presents the headline comparison, and three
readings matter. First, the privacy cost is modest where it counts
most: FedProx's ROC-AUC of 0.930 retains 95.0\% of the centralised
XGBoost upper bound (0.978), and its F1 of 0.326 retains 77.6\% of
XGBoost's 0.420---the model that no agency could have trained alone
achieves most of the discrimination of the model that required pooling
everyone's data. Second, the two federated algorithms are not
interchangeable: FedAvg collapses to a degenerate always-positive
classifier (precision 0.019, equal to the base rate; recall 1.000) and
even threshold optimisation cannot rescue it because its ranking
quality itself is poor (AUC 0.664). Third, logistic regression shows
the complementary failure of chasing recall without ranking skill:
with balanced class weights it fires on 57\% of all negatives,
halving accuracy for a recall of 0.838 that its AUC (0.818) barely
supports. The XGBoost baseline, by contrast, combines the strongest
ranking (AUC 0.978) with a threshold that converts it into genuinely
balanced detection (precision 0.282 at recall 0.821)---this is the
behaviour a federated replacement must approximate.

\subsection{Discrimination quality: ROC analysis}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \begin{adjustbox}{max width=0.72\textwidth, max height=0.42\textheight}
        \includegraphics{figures/ROC_Curves.png}
    \end{adjustbox}
    \caption{ROC curves on the held-out test set. FedProx (AUC 0.930)
    tracks the centralised XGBoost (AUC 0.978) across most of the
    operating range, while FedAvg (AUC 0.664) is barely separable from
    the logistic-regression baseline (AUC 0.818) and far from the
    random-chance diagonal.}
    \label{fig:roc}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:roc} separates what federation costs from what
aggregation choice costs. The FedProx curve hugs the XGBoost curve
over the low-false-positive region that matters operationally---a grid
operator cannot act on an alert that fires on a large fraction of all
active regions---and the residual AUC gap (0.048) is the price of (a)
training on six disjoint, label-skewed shards, (b) averaging neural
weights rather than optimising pooled data directly, and (c) a smaller
effective capacity than the 300-tree ensemble. FedAvg's curve, by
contrast, sags toward the diagonal: averaged updates from
heterogeneous clients have progressively destroyed the ranking
information that individual clients could learn locally. This is the
empirical signature of the client-drift mechanism that FedProx's
proximal term exists to suppress, and it is the reason we describe
FedProx, not federation in the abstract, as the load-bearing design
choice.

\subsection{Convergence behaviour and calibration}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \begin{adjustbox}{max width=0.96\textwidth, max height=0.34\textheight}
        \includegraphics{figures/FL_Convergence.png}
    \end{adjustbox}
    \caption{Federated convergence over 50 communication rounds,
    evaluated every five rounds at the fixed development threshold of
    0.35. Left: F1-score. Right: recall. Both federated models maintain
    recall $\approx$1.0 with near-degenerate F1 throughout---the
    signature of always-positive prediction under the
    train/test prior mismatch---which motivates the post-hoc
    threshold optimisation stage.}
    \label{fig:convergence}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:convergence} records an instructive failure mode
before the rescue. During training, both federated models are
evaluated at the fixed development threshold $\tau = 0.35$, and both
sit flat for all 50 rounds: recall pinned at 1.000, F1 pinned near
0.037. This is not non-convergence---the models converged in the
optimisation sense within the first evaluated rounds---but
\emph{miscalibration}: local training at a $\sim$49\% positive prior
(reinforced by SMOTE and the clamped focal loss) produces posterior
probabilities that are enormous relative to the natural 1.9\% prior,
so every test window clears the 0.35 threshold. The training-time
curves therefore measure the wrong thing, and the framework's
$F_\beta$ threshold sweep is what converts the converged models into
usable detectors: for FedProx the sweep selects $\tau^\ast = 0.80$,
trading half of the always-positive recall (1.000 $\to$ 0.416) for a
fourteen-fold precision gain (0.019 $\to$ 0.268). For FedAvg the sweep
runs to the boundary of its search range ($\tau^\ast = 0.10$) without
finding any precision--recall trade-off worth making---the model ranks
so poorly that no threshold helps. The lesson generalises to any
federated deployment trained on rebalanced local data: convergence
diagnostics at a fixed threshold are uninformative, and threshold
re-optimisation at deployment time is mandatory.

\subsection{Confusion matrices and the operational trade-off}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \begin{adjustbox}{max width=\textwidth, max height=0.36\textheight}
        \includegraphics{figures/Confusion_Matrices.png}
    \end{adjustbox}
    \caption{Row-normalised confusion matrices at each model's
    $F_\beta$-optimal threshold. FedProx achieves the second-lowest
    false-alarm rate (2\% of negatives) at the cost of missing 58\% of
    flares; FedAvg labels every window positive; XGBoost misses 18\% of
    flares at a 4\% false-alarm rate.}
    \label{fig:cm}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:cm} exposes the decision structure behind the table.
XGBoost's confusion matrix is operationally excellent: it retains 82\%
of flares while falsely alerting on only 4\% of quiet regions---a
false-alarm load an operator desk could actually triage. FedProx's
matrix shows the same conservative shape at reduced sensitivity: a 2\%
false-alarm rate (the most conservative of all four models) with 42\%
of flares retained. In GIC early warning, a model with a 2\% false-alarm
rate that catches 42\% of M/X events is not yet a primary forecast
instrument, but it is precisely the profile of a \emph{sentinel}: it
fires rarely, and when it fires the grid can afford to take the
expensive precautions. FedAvg's matrix---all predictions
positive---and logistic regression's 57\% false-alarm rate bracket the
two failure directions that the recall-weighted objective must steer
between. We return to the operational interpretation in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

Figure~\ref{fig:shap} and Table~\ref{tab:shap} audit the physics
content of the learned representation. Two patterns stand out. First,
the identity of the top features is exactly where flare physics says
it should be: the R-value flux-emergence proxy of
Schrijver~\cite{schrijver2007} appears three times in the top six in
different statistical guises, current-helicity and net-current
parameters (TOTUSJH, SAVNCPP, ABSNJZH, MEANJZD) dominate the top ten,
and Lorentz-force variability (TOTFX) enters at rank two---these are
the quantities that quantify magnetic free energy, current systems,
and non-potential stress in active regions \cite{bobra2015,leka2019}.
Second, and less expected, the \emph{statistics} matter as much as the
parameters: the ranking is led not by means but by extrema
(minimum R-value, maximum TOTUSJH) and standard deviations. The
minimum of the R-value over a 60-minute window measures how low the
flux-emergence activity dips before an eruption---an emergent-flux
baseline---while its maximum measures the peak emergence rate; their
joint dominance suggests the model keys on the \emph{contrast} between
quiet and active phases within the window, which is consistent with
the picture of flux emergence episodes precipitating
complexity growth. The strong showing of standard deviations
(TOTFX, MEANJZD) points to volatility---magnetic-field
fluctuation---as a flare precursor in its own right, in line with the
temporal-dynamics findings of recent sequence models
\cite{hassani2025,eskandari2024}. We caveat that the SHAP surrogate is
the centralised XGBoost rather than a federated model, and that the
pipeline's generic feature naming is itself a finding: the
interpretability layer of a federated deployment should preserve the
statistic-parameter naming from the outset (this is an engineering fix
we recommend in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Privacy-cost analysis}

Quantifying ``how much accuracy data sovereignty costs'' requires
decomposing the FedProx-to-XGBoost gap (F1 0.326 vs 0.420; AUC 0.930
vs 0.978). Three factors contribute, and only the first is
intrinsic to privacy: (i)~\emph{federation itself}---the best possible
federated model is still trained on six shards whose union is never
jointly optimised, and the averaging of client optima introduces
irreducible slack under label skew; (ii)~\emph{model-class
mismatch}---the fair pooled upper bound for an MLP would be a
centralised MLP, not a 300-tree gradient-boosting ensemble, so part of
the gap is architecture, not privacy; (iii)~\emph{recalled calibration
artifacts}---the always-positive training behaviour
(Fig.~\ref{fig:convergence}) indicates the local training regime has
not fully converged to a well-shaped posterior, leaving ranking skill
on the table. Ablating (ii) is straightforward future work---train the
centralised MLP with identical hyperparameters---and our expectation,
supported by the AUC gap being smaller than the F1 gap (4.8\% vs
22.4\% relative), is that the intrinsic privacy cost is closer to the
former. For the grid-protection use case, the honest summary is:
federation at this benchmark's scale costs roughly one fifth of F1
skill and one twentieth of ranking skill, in exchange for zero raw
data leaving any agency.
